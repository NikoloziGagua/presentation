# AI Usage Statement

**Tool used:** Claude (an AI assistant by Anthropic).

## How I used it

1. **Poster layout and design.** AI helped build the poster: the A1 page, the 2×2 panel layout, the colour scheme, the icons and the big-number strip under the title. It also drafted the short text in each panel from my sources, which I then checked and edited.
2. **Ideas for visuals.** I asked what visuals would explain MCAS to developers. It suggested comparing the 0.6° limit the FAA reviewed with the 2.5° that actually flew (Figure 3) and showing MCAS as simplified code (Figure 2). It also suggested what kinds of photos to look for.
3. **Structuring the video script.** I wasn't happy with how my first script flowed, so I asked AI to restructure it as a story. I then reworded it to sound like me.

## What I did myself

- **Researching MCAS and the crashes.** I read up on how MCAS worked, why it relied on one angle-of-attack sensor, and what the Lion Air and Ethiopian pilots actually did in the cockpit. That included the fact that the Ethiopian crew followed Boeing's procedure and still couldn't recover.
- **The argument.** I chose the topic and the "bug or business decision?" question. I also decided the answer: MCAS **was** a bug, because software can match its specification and still do something nobody intended. I changed the script and poster to argue that, after the first AI draft had said it wasn't a bug.
- **Directing the design.** I set the requirements and rejected versions that didn't meet them: the poster had to be A1, the header photo was stretched, and the top had too much text. I pushed for a more visual poster with less text, which is what our lecturer asks for.
- **Photos and sources.** I found and chose the photos myself, and I found extra sources, such as a YouTube video, which I referenced.
- **Checking the script.** I caught wording that didn't make sense, such as the line about Boeing's $1 million promise to Southwest, and had it made clearer.
- **Fact-checking.** I checked the numbers and dates against the House committee report, the Seattle Times and the AP timeline, and only cited those.
- **The video.** I recorded the voice-over and edited the video myself.

## Reflection: how useful was it?

It was very useful for the visual side. Laying out a poster with this many elements by hand would have taken me days, and AI got me to something with more images and less text much faster.

It was less reliable on detail. The first versions had overflowing text, the wrong orientation and a stretched photo, so I had to review everything and ask for fixes. It also took a position on the main question that I disagreed with, which showed me it can sound confident without having thought it through.

The biggest lesson was that AI is fast at making something *look* finished, but I still had to understand the topic well enough to know whether it was right. That's also the point of the poster: something doing exactly what it was told isn't the same as it being correct.
